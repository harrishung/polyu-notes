=== WHAT IS A BANK? ===

A bank performs as a FINANCIAL INTERMEDIARY that:
- Accepts deposits from customers
- Makes loans to borrowers
- Provides payment services
- Offers financial advisory services

Key Characteristics:
- Transforms short-term deposits into long-term loans
- Manages credit and liquidity risk
- Provides essential financial services
- Subject to government regulation and supervision

--- The Many Roles Banks Play (Table 1-1) ---
- Intermediation role: transforms household savings into credit (loans) for businesses
- Payment role: clears checks, provides electronic payment conduits
- Guarantor role: stands behind customers (e.g., letters of credit)
- Risk management role: helps customers prepare for risk of loss
- Investment banking role: assists corporations/governments in raising new funds
- Savings/investment advisor role: aids customers in long-range financial goals
- Safekeeping/certification role: safeguards valuables, certifies value
- Agency role: acts on behalf of customers to manage property
- Policy role: serves as conduit for government economic policy

--- Different Kinds of Financial-Service Firms Called Banks (Exhibit 1-1) ---
- Commercial banks: deposits and loans to businesses, individuals, institutions
- Money Centre Banks: largest commercial banks in leading financial centers
- Community Banks: smaller, locally focused commercial and savings banks
- Savings banks: attract savings deposits, lend to individuals and families
- Cooperative banks: serve farmers, ranchers, consumers
- Mortgage banks: provide mortgage loans, do not sell deposits
- Investment banks: underwrite new securities for corporate customers
- Merchant banks: supply debt and equity capital to businesses
- Industrial banks: state-chartered loan companies owned by other corporations
- International banks: commercial banks present in more than one nation
- Wholesale banks: larger commercial banks serving corporations and governments
- Retail banks: smaller banks serving households and small businesses
- Limited-purpose banks: narrow menu (credit card companies, subprime lenders)
- Bankers' banks: supply services (e.g., check clearing) to other banks
- Minority banks: focus on minority group customers
- National banks: federal charter via Comptroller of the Currency (US)
- State banks: charters issued by state banking commissions
- Insured banks: deposits backed by federal deposit insurance (e.g., FDIC)
- Member banks: belong to the Federal Reserve System
- Affiliated banks: wholly or partially owned by a holding company
- Virtual banks: services only over the Internet
- Fringe banks: payday/title loans, check cashing, pawn shops
- Universal banks: offer virtually all financial services

--- Money-Center vs Community Banks ---
Money-center banks:
- Industry leaders
- Cover whole regions, nations, and continents
- Offer the widest possible menu of financial services
- Acquire smaller businesses
- Face tough global competition

Community banks:
- Much smaller
- Service local communities and towns
- Offer narrower but more personalized menu

US examples:
- Money-center: JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America
- Community: First Citizens Bank (Raleigh, NC), Eastern Bank (Boston, MA)

HK examples:
- Money-center: HSBC, Bank of China (HK) Limited (BOCHK)
- Community: Hang Seng Bank, Dah Sing Bank

--- Legal Basis for Banking ---
- A bank is any business offering deposits subject to withdrawal on demand and making loans of a commercial or business nature
- US Congress later defined a bank as any institution qualifying for FDIC deposit insurance
- HK equivalent of FDIC = Hong Kong Deposit Protection Scheme (DPS), administered by the Hong Kong Deposit Protection Board (HKDPB)

=== BANKING REGULATION AND HISTORY ===

--- Historical Timeline of Major US Banking Laws ---

1863-1864: National Currency and Bank Acts
- First major federal banking laws (Civil War era)
- Established national banking system
- Created Office of Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) inside US Treasury
- OCC charters and examines national banks

1913: Federal Reserve Act
- Created the Federal Reserve System (the Fed) in response to financial panics
- Central bank for the United States
- Fed's principal roles: lender of last resort, stabilize financial markets and economy
- Today: control money and credit conditions to promote economic stability

1933: Banking Act (Glass-Steagall)
Key Provisions:
1. Separated Commercial and Investment Banking
   - Commercial banks: deposits and loans
   - Investment banks: securities underwriting
2. Created FDIC (deposit insurance)
   - Initially insured up to $2,500 per depositor
   - Now insures up to $250,000
3. Prohibited Certain Activities
   - Banks couldn't underwrite securities
   - Investment banks couldn't take deposits
Impact: Cornerstone of US banking regulation for 66 years

1956: Bank Holding Company Act
- Regulated bank holding companies

--- Deregulation Era (1980s-1990s) ---

1980: Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act (DIDMCA)
- Phased out interest rate ceilings
- Allowed thrifts broader services

1982: Garn-St Germain Act
- Money market deposit accounts
- Wider asset investment authority

1994: Riegle-Neal Interstate Banking Law
- Allowed interstate banking
- Enabled national banking system

1999: Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act
- Repealed Glass-Steagall
- Allowed financial supermarkets

--- The Savings and Loan Crisis (1986-1995) ---

Causes:
- Interest rate deregulation
- Risky lending practices
- Weak regulatory oversight
- Real estate speculation

Consequences:
- Over 1,000 S&Ls failed
- $125 billion government bailout
- Massive taxpayer cost
- Unemployment and economic disruption

Regulatory Response:
- FIRREA (1989): restructured regulation
- FDICIA (1991): enhanced oversight

--- Post-2008 Regulatory Framework ---

The Dodd-Frank Act (2010) Key Provisions:
- Created Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)
- Established Volcker Rule (proprietary trading ban)
- Required stress testing
- Enhanced capital requirements
- Orderly liquidation authority

Regulatory Agencies:
- Federal Reserve
- OCC (Office of Comptroller of the Currency)
- FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation)
- CFPB (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau)
- SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission)


=== CENTRAL BANKING AND MONETARY POLICY ===

--- The Federal Reserve System Structure ---

1. Board of Governors (Washington, D.C.)
   - 7 members, 14-year terms
   - Appointed by President, confirmed by Senate

2. Twelve Regional Federal Reserve Banks
   - Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Cleveland
   - Richmond, Atlanta, Chicago, St. Louis
   - Minneapolis, Kansas City, Dallas, San Francisco

3. Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)
   - 7 Board members + 5 Regional Bank presidents
   - Sets monetary policy

--- Three Main Monetary Policy Tools ---

1. Open Market Operations (OMO)
   - Buying and selling government securities
   - Most flexible tool, can be easily reversed
   - Most frequently used

2. Discount Rate
   - Interest rate the Fed charges banks
   - Less frequently changed
   - Signals Fed policy intentions

3. Reserve Requirements
   - Percentage of deposits banks must hold
   - Rarely changed (powerful impact)
   - Eliminated for most banks in 2020

--- How Monetary Policy Affects the Economy ---
Transmission Mechanism:
1. Fed Action: changes in OMO, discount rate, or reserve requirements
2. Bank Behavior: changes in reserves affect lending capacity
3. Interest Rates: market interest rates adjust
4. Borrowing and Spending: consumers and businesses respond
5. Economic Activity: GDP, employment, and inflation affected
Time Lag: 6-18 months for full effect

--- The Federal Funds Rate ---
Definition:
- Interest rate on overnight loans between banks
- Banks lend reserve balances to each other
- Fed sets target range

Importance:
- Primary indicator of Fed policy stance
- Affects all other interest rates
- Closely watched by financial markets

FOMC Actions:
- Raises rate to fight inflation
- Lowers rate to stimulate the economy
- Communicates through rate changes
- Provides forward guidance

--- The Central Banking System (Decisions and Policies) ---
- US central bank = Federal Reserve System (the Fed)
- Central bank's primary job = monetary policy
- Goal: ensure economy grows adequately, unemployment low, inflation held down
- Fed is independent (does not depend on government for funding); passes earnings to US Treasury
- ECB (European Central Bank) is also relatively independent
- Bank of Japan (BOJ), People's Bank of China (PBC), and other Asian central banks appear under closer government control
- Research suggests more independent central banks better control inflation

--- Federal Reserve Bank Services to Depository Institutions ---
1. Issuing wire transfers between depository institutions
2. Safe-keeping securities owned by depository institutions and customers
3. Issuing new securities from US Treasury and federal agencies
4. Making loans through the Discount Window
5. Dispensing currency and coin
6. Clearing and collecting checks and other cash items
7. Providing information on developments

Member banks: all OCC-chartered (national) banks plus state banks willing to conform to Fed supervision. Members must purchase stock (up to 6% of paid-in capital and surplus) in their district Reserve bank and submit to comprehensive examinations.

--- Other Central Bank Policy Tools ---
Moral suasion:
- Central bank applies psychological pressure on individuals/institutions to conform to policy
Examples:
- Officials testifying before legislative committees
- Letters/calls to institutions straying from policy
- Press releases urging public cooperation

--- Leading Central Banks in Europe ---
1. European Central Bank (ECB): central bank for Eurozone (19 of 27 EU member states)
2. Bank of England (BoE): UK central bank, issues GBP
3. Deutsche Bundesbank: Germany's central bank, key Eurosystem institution
4. Banque de France: France's central bank, integral to Eurosystem

--- Leading Central Banks in Asia ---
1. People's Bank of China (PBOC): China's central bank
2. Bank of Japan (BoJ): Japan's central bank, issues JPY, known for unconventional monetary easing
3. Reserve Bank of India (RBI): India's central bank, issues INR


=== FINANCIAL SYSTEM OVERVIEW ===

--- Financial Services in 1950 (Specialized Institution Model) ---
Commercial Banks: deposits, commercial lending, payment services, limited geography
Investment Firms: securities underwriting, stock brokerage
Insurance Companies: life and property insurance, savings products
Pension Funds: retirement savings, long-term investing
Result: Little overlap or competition

--- Products of US Financial Institutions in 1950 ---
(Functions: Payment Services, Savings Products, Fiduciary Services, Lending [Business/Consumer], Underwriting [Equity/Debt], Insurance & Risk Mgmt)
- Depository Institutions: Payment, Savings, Fiduciary, Business Lending, Consumer Lending
- Insurance Companies: Savings, Business Lending, Insurance
- Finance Companies: Business Lending (minor), Consumer Lending, Insurance
- Securities Firms: Savings, Fiduciary, Business Lending (minor), Equity Underwriting, Debt Underwriting
- Pension Funds: Savings
- Mutual Funds: Savings

--- Financial Services in 2021 (Convergence Model) ---
Financial Supermarkets offer:
- Banking services
- Investment services
- Insurance products
- Wealth management
- All from one institution

Examples: JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citigroup
Result: Significant overlap and competition
Challenge: Increased complexity and systemic risk

--- Products of US Financial Institutions in 2021 ---
- Depository Institutions: ALL functions (payment, savings, fiduciary, all lending, all underwriting, insurance)
- Insurance Companies: ALL functions
- Finance Companies: most functions; equity/debt underwriting minor
- Securities Firms: ALL functions
- Pension Funds: savings, fiduciary, business lending, insurance
- Mutual Funds: payment, savings, fiduciary


=== BANKING REGULATIONS IN DETAIL ===

--- FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) ---
Created: 1933 (Banking Act)
Purpose:
- Insure deposits of bank customers
- Prevent bank runs and panics
- Maintain public confidence
Coverage:
- Up to $250,000 per depositor per bank
- Covers checking, savings, money market
- Does NOT cover stocks, bonds, mutual funds
Funding:
- Premiums paid by member banks
- Risk-based pricing system
- Reserve fund for payouts

--- US Bank Regulators (Primary Federal) ---
1. Office of Comptroller of Currency (OCC)
   - Charters national banks
   - Examines for safety and soundness
   - Part of Treasury Department
2. Federal Reserve
   - Regulates state member banks
   - Supervises bank holding companies
   - Conducts monetary policy
3. FDIC
   - Insures deposits
   - Regulates state non-member banks
   - Acts as receiver for failed banks
4. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)
   - Enforces consumer protection laws
   - Handles consumer complaints

--- Bank Capital Requirements (Basel III, Post-2008) ---
Definition: equity capital banks must maintain as buffer against losses, expressed as percentage of assets.

Minimum standards:
- Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1): 4.5%
- Tier 1 Capital: 6%
- Total Capital: 8%

Additional Requirements:
- Capital conservation buffer: 2.5%
- Countercyclical buffer: 0-2.5%
- G-SIB surcharge: 1-3.5%

Purpose: absorb losses, maintain lending during stress, reduce systemic risk

--- Consumer Protection Laws ---
Truth in Lending Act (TILA):
- Requires clear disclosure of loan terms (APR, finance charges, payment schedule)

Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA):
- Prohibits discrimination in lending (race, color, religion, national origin)

Community Reinvestment Act (CRA):
- Requires banks to serve all communities
- Prohibits redlining
- Affects regulatory approval of mergers

Dodd-Frank Act (2010):
- Created CFPB
- Requires plain language in disclosures
- Prohibits unfair and deceptive practices


=== THE 2008 FINANCIAL CRISIS ===

Timeline: 2007-2009

Causes:
- Subprime mortgage lending boom
- Complex securities (MBS, CDO)
- Excessive leverage
- Inadequate regulation
- Rating agency failures

Key Events:
- Bear Stearns collapse (March 2008)
- Lehman Brothers failure (September 2008)
- AIG bailout ($182 billion)
- Credit market freeze
- Stock market crash (57% decline)

Consequences:
- 9 million jobs lost
- $16 trillion in household wealth destroyed
- Unemployment reached 10%
