=== SOUND RISK MANAGEMENT ===
===========================================================

=== ELEMENTS OF A SOUND RISK MANAGEMENT SYSTEM ===
(Source: HKMA Supervisory Policy Manual – IC1)

CORE PILLARS OF THE FRAMEWORK:
1. Risk Governance
2. Risk Appetite Framework (RAF)
3. Three Lines of Defence

1. RISK GOVERNANCE:
Formal structures defining Board & Senior Management responsibilities for
strategy, risk appetite, and control.
- Includes a clear segregation of duties for risk management and control

2. RISK APPETITE FRAMEWORK (RAF):
Policies & processes to establish, communicate, and monitor the institution's
risk tolerance.

CRITICAL SUCCESS FACTORS:
1. Top-Down Tone: Set by an engaged Board and Senior Management
2. Strong Risk Culture: Promoting awareness, open communication, and accountability
3. Firm-Wide Application: RM must be embedded across all business activities and entities


=== THREE LINES OF DEFENCE ===

FIRST LINE OF DEFENCE — Business Units:
- Role: Own and manage risk daily
- Responsibility: Identify, assess, and report risks within approved policies and limits

SECOND LINE OF DEFENCE — Independent Risk & Compliance Functions:
- Risk Management Unit: Oversees risk-taking, sets frameworks, monitors limits,
  reports independently
- Compliance Unit: Ensures adherence to laws, regulations, and internal policies

THIRD LINE OF DEFENCE — Internal Audit Function:
- Role: Provides independent assurance
- Responsibility: Audits effectiveness of the entire RM framework and the first two lines


=== EIGHT + ONE KEY RISK CATEGORIES (HKMA INHERENT RISKS) ===

1. CREDIT RISK: Risk of borrower or counterparty default
2. INTEREST RATE RISK: Risk from adverse movements in interest rates affecting the banking book
3. MARKET RISK: Risk of losses from movements in market prices (FX, equities, commodities)
4. LIQUIDITY RISK: Risk of being unable to meet obligations as they fall due
5. OPERATIONAL RISK: Risk of loss from inadequate processes, people, systems, or external events
6. LEGAL RISK: Risk of loss from legal actions, penalties, or unenforceable contracts
7. REPUTATION RISK: Risk of loss from damage to public perception and trust
8. STRATEGIC RISK (Business Risk): Risk from poor business decisions, execution, or failure to adapt

=== OPERATIONAL RISK ===
===========================================================

=== DEFINITION ===
Operational risk is present in virtually all bank transactions and activities.

Defined by Basel Committee under Basel II as:
"the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people
and systems or from external events"

This definition INCLUDES legal risk but EXCLUDES strategic and reputational risk.

(Source: HKMA OR-1 — https://www.hkma.gov.hk/media/eng/doc/key-functions/banking-stability/supervisory-policy-manual/OR-1.pdf)

=== 1. PEOPLE ===
Examples:
- Intentional and illegal breaches of policies and rules
- Sloppy execution
- Lack of knowledge and training
- Failures to comply with a professional obligation to clients
- Improper business and market practices


=== 2. SYSTEMS AND TECHNOLOGY ===
Examples:
- Hardware failure
- Software failure
- Systems failure
- Utility outage
- Business disruptions

CASE STUDY — DBS BANK DISRUPTIONS (2023):
DBS CEO Piyush Gupta said the bank is "deeply sorry" for the digital disruptions.
DBS experienced major disruptions to banking services on:
- 29 March 2023
- 5 May 2023
- 26 September 2023
- 14 October 2023
- 20 October 2023

MAS RESPONSE (Singapore, 1 November 2023):
- MAS imposed a SIX-MONTH PAUSE on DBS Bank's non-essential IT changes
- DBS not allowed to acquire new business ventures during this period
- DBS not allowed to reduce size of branch and ATM networks in Singapore

April 2023: MAS directed DBS to engage independent third-party for comprehensive review.
Shortcomings identified in:
- System resilience
- Incident management
- Change management
- Technology risk governance and oversight

Following independent review, DBS set out a technology resiliency roadmap
implemented in phases (changes to system architecture design take more time).

MAS reviewed and was satisfied with scope and planned measures.
DBS will hold senior management accountable for lapses; board will enhance
governance approach to oversee implementation.

MAS retained multiplier of 1.8× to DBS's risk weighted assets for operational risk
(imposed after March and May 2023 incidents).

It will take up to 24 months for DBS to put in place planned structural changes
to improve resilience of digital banking services. In the meantime, disruptions may
still occur — MAS expects DBS to promptly recover services and communicate to
customers in a clear and timely manner.


=== 3. PROCESSES AND POLICIES ===
Examples:
- Aggressive sales targets and incentives
- Unclear and sometimes contradictory procedures
- Employment discrimination

SIX GUIDING PRINCIPLES OF INCENTIVE DESIGN
(for reinforcing good customer and conduct outcomes):
a. Clear prioritisation of customer outcomes
b. Development of an aligned system
c. Focusing on intrinsic motivators
d. Wise use of technology
e. Close connection between leaders and staff
f. Minimising potential misconduct and mis-selling behaviours

SIXTEEN SOUND PRACTICES (Build Sound Practices):
1. Rewarding investment in long-term relationships
2. Aligning internal policy with values
3. Reinforcing customer outcomes over solely fulfilling customer requests
4. Increased focus on customer-oriented behaviour, not sales
5. Supporting staff to understand customer needs
6. Customer outcomes over process compliance
7. Integrating compliance requirements into system design
8. Fair and objective monitoring of conduct
9. Responding to staff feedback
10. Rewarding non-financial performance
11. Reducing incentive system complexity
12. Setting dynamic targets
13. Managing behavioural risk from tactical incentive programs
14. Clear and appropriate consequences for undesired behaviour
15. Considering diverse perspectives
16. Amplifying good conduct with social rewards


=== 4. EXTERNAL EVENTS ===
Examples:
- Natural disasters
- Terrorist attacks
- External theft and fraud
- Robbery
- Cybercrime
- Regulatory changes


=== OPERATIONAL RISK MANAGEMENT FRAMEWORK (ORMF) ===

According to BIS, key elements of the ORMF are:
1. Governance
2. The risk management environment
3. Information and communication technology
4. Business continuity planning
5. The role of disclosure

An effective ORMF can be achieved through a process involving:
- Governance structure
- Operational risk identification, assessment, measurement methodologies
- Policies, procedures, and processes for mitigating, controlling, monitoring,
  and reporting of operational risks

=== INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY RISKS ===
===========================================================

According to Deloitte, the most significant risks in technology in financial services:
- Strategic risk of Information Technology (IT)
- Cybersecurity risk
- Technology vendor and third-party risk
- Data management risk
- Risk of ineffective management


=== STRATEGIC RISK OF IT ===
Risk emanating from errors in IT strategy stands among the top threats a Financial Institution faces.

Examples:
- Not embracing new technology
- Not upgrading legacy systems
- Not integrating IT and business strategies
- Investing in the wrong technology

2019 Gartner CIO Survey: highest-ranking technology expected to be game-changing
in the financial sector is AI and machine learning.


=== CYBERSECURITY RISK ===

Methods of attack:
- PHISHING: a primary method of breaching data systems
- WATERING HOLES: infecting websites frequently visited by targeted people
- DENIAL-OF-SERVICE (DoS) ATTACKS: deployment of large numbers of internet bots
  (hundreds to hundreds of thousands)

Example — DDoS Attack:
On March 12, 2012, six U.S. banks were targeted by a wave of DDoS attacks:
- Bank of America
- JPMorgan Chase
- U.S. Bank
- Citigroup
- Wells Fargo
- PNC Bank


=== TECHNOLOGY VENDOR AND THIRD-PARTY RISK ===

Third-party arrangements can help management achieve strategic objectives:
- Lower costs
- Increased revenues
- Expansion of customer base or product capabilities

Financial Institutions must develop and implement proper due diligence,
contracting, and monitoring procedures for ALL third parties, including
technology vendors engaged by IT.


=== DATA MANAGEMENT RISK ===

Definition:
Exposure to loss of value or trust caused by issues or limitations in an
organization's ability to acquire, store, transform, move, and use its data assets.

Management:
- Managed via advanced analytics techniques and leveraging cognitive technologies

Regulatory Example:
- EU's issuance of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)


=== RISK OF INEFFECTIVE RISK MANAGEMENT ===

Financial Institutions traditionally pursue a THREE LINES OF DEFENSE model:
1. Product and process owners — identify and manage risk
2. Risk and compliance — provides risk management structure and independent oversight of first line
3. Internal audit — provides independent assurance on effectiveness of first two lines
   to the board and senior management

=== EFFECTS OF TECHNOLOGY ON REVENUES AND COSTS ===
===========================================================

=== TECHNOLOGY INVESTMENT IS RISKY ===

- Potentially negative NPV projects due to uncertainties over revenues and costs
  and how quickly rivals will mimic or copy any innovation
- Agency conflicts may result in managers undertaking growth-oriented investments
  to increase a Financial Institution's size

PROFITABILITY OF PRODUCT INNOVATION:

NEGATIVELY related to:
- Size of the initial setup and development costs
- Financial Institution's cost of capital

POSITIVELY related to:
- Size of the stream of expected net cash flows from selling the services


=== TECHNOLOGY AND REVENUES ===
- Allows cross-selling of new and existing products to existing customers
- Increases the rate of innovation of new financial products
  (Growth areas: advanced data analytics, blockchain technology, FinTech challengers)
- Service quality and convenience


=== TECHNOLOGY AND COSTS ===
For larger Financial Institutions, scale and array of potential technology investments is greater.

Potential cost advantage:
- Economies of scale
- Potential elimination of smaller banks?

Risks:
- Potential excess capacity, integration and cost control problems
- Diseconomies of scale


=== ECONOMIES OF SCOPE / DISECONOMIES OF SCOPE ===

Economies of Scope:
- Joint use of inputs may provide synergistic cost savings in producing multiple products
- Financial Institution size may affect potential gains and losses from IT investments

Diseconomies of Scope:
- Costs of joint production of services are HIGHER than if independently produced

Wire Transfer Systems:

Combined value: often more than US$4.6 trillion per day.


=== WIRE TRANSFER SYSTEM RISKS ===

DAYLIGHT OVERDRAFT RISK:
- FedWire settlement at 6:30 Eastern Time (ET)
- Banks commonly run daylight overdrafts
- 50 basis point fee for daylight overdraft
- Until 1993, the Fed did not charge banks an explicit interest rate for daylight overdrafts
- Under Regulation J: Fed guarantees payment finality of wire transfer messages
  → Fed bears credit risk of bank failures
- Regulation F: sets exposure limits to individual correspondents

INTERNATIONAL TECHNOLOGY TRANSFER RISK

CRIME AND FRAUD RISK:
- Fraud risk, especially from Financial Institution employees
- Riggs National Bank transactions with Saudis
- Costs of complying with Patriot Act
  (USA PATRIOT Act = Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools
  Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism Act)


=== REGULATORY RISK ===

Technology facilitates avoidance of regulation by locating in least regulated state or country.

Examples:
- Citigroup credit card operations in South Dakota
- South Dakota and Delaware most liberal in usury ceilings and regulatory controls
- Cayman Islands is the world's eighth biggest banking center


=== ADDITIONAL RISKS ===

COMPETITION RISK:

- Car finance companies provide about a quarter of the market for car leases and loans

=== APPENDIX: BIS PRINCIPLES FOR SOUND MANAGEMENT OF OPERATIONAL RISK ===
===========================================================

PRINCIPLE 1:
The board of directors should take the lead in establishing a strong risk management culture.
The board and senior management should establish a corporate culture guided by strong
risk management that supports and provides appropriate standards and incentives for
professional and responsible behaviour. It is the responsibility of the board to ensure
that a strong operational risk management culture exists throughout the whole organisation.

PRINCIPLE 2:
Banks should develop, implement and maintain a Framework that is fully integrated into
the bank's overall risk management processes. The Framework chosen by an individual bank
will depend on its nature, size, complexity and risk profile.

PRINCIPLE 3:
The board of directors should establish, approve and periodically review the Framework.
The board should oversee senior management to ensure policies, processes and systems
are implemented effectively at all decision levels.

PRINCIPLE 4:
The board should approve and review a risk appetite and tolerance statement for
operational risk that articulates the nature, types and levels of operational risk
the bank is willing to assume.

PRINCIPLE 5:
Senior management should develop for board approval a clear, effective and robust
governance structure with well-defined, transparent and consistent lines of responsibility.
Senior management is responsible for consistently implementing and maintaining policies,
processes and systems for managing operational risk in all material products, activities,
processes and systems consistent with the risk appetite and tolerance.

PRINCIPLE 6:
Senior management should ensure the identification and assessment of operational risk
inherent in all material products, activities, processes and systems to ensure inherent
risks and incentives are well understood.

PRINCIPLE 7:
Senior management should ensure there is an approval process for all new products,
activities, processes and systems that fully assesses operational risk.

PRINCIPLE 8:
Senior management should implement a process to regularly monitor operational risk profiles
and material exposures to losses. Appropriate reporting mechanisms should be in place at
the board, senior management, and business line levels to support proactive management.

PRINCIPLE 9:
Banks should have a strong control environment that utilizes policies, processes and systems;
appropriate internal controls; and appropriate risk mitigation and/or transfer strategies.

PRINCIPLE 10:
Banks should have business resiliency and continuity plans in place to ensure an ability
to operate on an ongoing basis and limit losses in the event of severe business disruption.

PRINCIPLE 11:
A bank's public disclosures should allow stakeholders to assess its approach to
operational risk management.
