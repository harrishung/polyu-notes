=== INTRODUCTION ===
- Consumer debt has become one of the fastest-growing forms of borrowing
- Q2 2023: total US household debt reached US$17 Trillion; Credit Card Debt exceeded US$1 Trillion
- Banks dominate household lending due to growing reliance on individuals/families
  for chief source of funds (checkable and savings deposits)
- Consumer credit is among the most profitable services a lender can offer
- However, services directed at consumers can also be among the most COSTLY and RISKY


=== TYPES OF LOANS GRANTED TO INDIVIDUALS AND FAMILIES ===

Consumer loans classified by:
- PURPOSE: what the borrowed funds will be used for
- TYPE: whether borrower repays in installments or in one lump sum


=== RESIDENTIAL MORTGAGE LOANS ===

Definition:
- Credit to finance home purchase or fund residential improvements
- Long-term loan, typically 15-30 years
- Secured by the property itself
- May carry fixed or variable (floating) interest rate
- Banks are leading residential mortgage lenders today

Leading US Mortgage Lenders:
- Wells Fargo: largest US bank, significant mortgage market presence
- JPMorgan Chase: major US bank with substantial market share
- Quicken Loans (Rocket Mortgage): well-known online lender, streamlined digital application
- Bank of America: substantial residential lending presence
- Citibank (Citigroup subsidiary): notable player

Leading Hong Kong Mortgage Lenders:
- HSBC Hong Kong: largest, most prominent bank
- Bank of China (Hong Kong): major regional bank
- Standard Chartered Bank (Hong Kong): residents and non-residents
- Hang Seng Bank (HSBC subsidiary): competitive rates


=== NON-RESIDENTIAL CONSUMER LOANS ===

INSTALLMENT LOANS:
- Short to medium-term, repayable in 2+ consecutive payments (monthly/quarterly)
- Used for big-ticket items (automobiles, furniture, appliances) or debt consolidation

NON-INSTALLMENT LOANS:
- Short-term loans for immediate cash needs, repayable in lump sum
- May be small amounts, including charge accounts requiring 30-day or less payment
- May also be larger short-term loans (under 6 months) to wealthier individuals


=== CREDIT CARD LOANS AND REVOLVING CREDIT ===

One of the most popular forms of consumer credit today.

Benefits to Consumers:
- Convenience: easy way to make purchases
- Access to Credit: can purchase even without sufficient cash
- Rewards programs, cashback incentives, travel perks
- Security: fraud protection and ability to dispute charges

Concerns:
- Customers were charged high fees but encouraged to make only low minimum payments
- Resulted in NEGATIVE AMORTIZATION
- Regulators warned lenders that federal examiners would look for excessive use
  of fees and unreasonably liberal credit terms


=== NEW CONSUMER REGULATIONS — DODD-FRANK, CARD ACT, AND CFPB ===

CARD ACT (May 2009) — Credit Card Accountability, Responsibility, and Disclosure Act:
- Restricted card issuers from raising APRs unless adequate written notice given
- Customers must be told reasons why credit terms changed
- Card companies required to post contracts on Internet for customers to "shop around"
- Card holders must receive periodic billing statements at least 3 weeks before payments due
- Expanded "box" must be included on each monthly billing statement showing:
  - Amount of interest paid
  - Consequences of paying minimum amount
- Falls within Federal Reserve Board's Regulation Z

DODD-FRANK WALL STREET REFORM AND CONSUMER PROTECTION ACT:
- Named after Senator Chris Dodd and Congressman Barney Frank
- Signed into law by President Obama in July 2010
- Creates the CONSUMER FINANCIAL PROTECTION BUREAU (CFPB)
- CFPB directed to write new rules on:
  - Making consumer and credit card loans
  - Warning consumers of damaging financial practices
  - Promoting financial literacy
  - Improving clarity/transparency of financial-service contracts
- CFPB housed within Federal Reserve but operates independently with own budget
- Expected to be controversial — must write hundreds of rules impacting consumer services


=== CHARACTERISTICS OF CONSUMER LOANS ===

- Lenders regard consumer loans as profitable credits with "STICKY" interest rates
- Contract rates often don't change readily with market conditions (unlike business loans)
- Consumer loans subject to significant interest rate risk
- Priced so high that market rates and default rates would have to rise substantially
  before consumer credits become unprofitable

Why are interest rates so high on most consumer loans?
- Most costly and risky to make per dollar of loanable funds committed
- Consumer loans tend to be cyclically sensitive
- Household borrowings appear relatively interest INELASTIC
  - Borrowers more concerned about size of monthly payment than rate charged
- Education and income levels materially influence consumers' use of credit


=== EVALUATING A CONSUMER LOAN APPLICATION ===

Character and Purpose:
- Key factors: character of borrower and ability to pay
- Lenders nearly always check with credit bureaus for credit history
- For borrowers without credit record or with poor history, COSIGNER may be requested
- Many lenders regard cosigner as primarily a psychological device to encourage repayment

Other Important Items For Lenders:
- Income Levels
- Deposit Balances
- Employment and Residential Stability
- Pyramiding of Debt

How to Qualify for a Consumer Loan:
- Home ownership or ownership of any form of real property
- Maintain strong deposit balances
- Most important: TRUTHFULLY answer all of the loan officer's questions


=== CREDIT SCORING ===

Basic Theory:
Lenders and statisticians can identify financial, economic, and motivational factors
that separate good loans from bad loans.

Underlying Assumption:
Same factors that separated good from bad loans in the past will separate them
in the future, within an acceptable risk of error.

Effect: Automated credit-determining system removes personal judgment from lending.


=== THE FICO SYSTEM ===

- Developed by Fair Isaac Corporation (FICO)
- Most famous credit-scoring system in use
- Scores range 300-850; higher = less credit risk

FICO score based on five factors (most to least important):
1. Borrower's payment history
2. Amount of money owed
3. Length of borrower's credit history
4. Nature of new credit being requested
5. Types of credit borrower has already used


=== LAWS AND REGULATIONS APPLYING TO CONSUMER LOANS ===

Two categories:

1. DISCLOSURE RULES:
   Mandate telling consumer about cost and other terms of loan/lease agreement
   - Truth-in-Lending Act
   - Fair Credit Reporting Act
   - Fair Credit Billing Act
   - Fair Debt Collection Practices Act

2. ANTI-DISCRIMINATION LAWS:
   Prevent categorizing customers by age, sex, race, etc.
   - Equal Credit Opportunity Act
   - Community Reinvestment Act


=== PREDATORY LENDING AND SUBPRIME ===

Predatory Lending: abusive practice where lenders require excessive fees
plus unnecessary and excessive loan insurance.

Subprime Loans: granting loans to borrowers with below-average credit scores.

Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act (1994):
Passed to protect home buyers from loan agreements they could not afford.
Subprime lending is difficult to regulate.


=== REAL ESTATE LOANS ===

- Depository institutions, finance, and insurance companies make real estate loans
  to fund acquisition of real property (homes, apartments, shopping centers, offices, land)
- One of the most rapidly growing areas of lending over past decade
- Different from other loans
- Can be among the riskiest forms of credit


=== DIFFERENCES BETWEEN REAL ESTATE LOANS AND OTHER LOANS ===

- Average size much larger than other loans
- Mortgage loans tend to have longer maturities
  - 15-30 year maturities typical for single-family homes
- Condition and value of subject property are nearly as important as borrower's income
  - Appraisals critical to loan decision; must meet industry standards and government regulations


=== FACTORS IN EVALUATING REAL ESTATE LOAN APPLICATIONS ===

- DOWN PAYMENT relative to purchase price:
  Higher loan-to-purchase price ratio → less incentive to honor loan terms
- Lenders may give lower rate for pledge to use other financial services
- Other aspects:
  - Amount and stability of borrower's income
  - Available savings and source of down payment
  - Track record in caring for/managing property
  - Outlook for real estate sales in local market (in case of repossession)
  - Outlook for market interest rates


=== HOME EQUITY LENDING ===

- Homeowners can borrow the equity in their homes
- Equity = home's estimated market value minus the mortgage loans against it

Two main types:
1. Traditional Home Equity Loan
2. Lines of Credit Against a Home's Borrowing Base


=== CONTROVERSIAL HOME MORTGAGE LOANS — INTEREST-ONLY AND ARMS ===

During the housing boom:
- Lenders made extravagantly priced homes "affordable" via more readily available mortgages
- Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs) signed up during historic-low rate period

Interest-Only ARM (Option ARM):
- Home buyer pays only interest on home loan for initial period
- After initial period, must pay both principal and interest
- Looked like predatory lending against lower-income families
- In rising rate environment, many ARM borrowers faced higher payments

Post-Crisis Reforms:
- Lenders must disclose more about actual loan terms
- Cannot represent loan terms as "fixed" when those terms can change
- Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act resulted in tough new rules:
  - Lenders pooling/securitizing mortgages they create are responsible for at least
    5 percent of attached credit risk (qualified mortgages exempt)
  - Previously lenders "washed their hands" of any responsibility


=== REVISED FEDERAL BANKRUPTCY CODE ===

Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act (April 2005, Bush):
- Made filing for bankruptcy more expensive and time-consuming
- Applicants must complete a certified credit counseling program before filing
- Intended to discourage consumers from taking on too much debt

Means Test (determines Chapter 7 vs Chapter 13):
- Average of debtor's past six months of gross income
- Determines if debtor has enough income to pay some debt

Chapter 7 (previous default):
- Wiped out all or most debts; allowed fresh start

Chapter 13 (more applicants must use under new code):
- Stipulates that at least some debts must be repaid


=== PRICING CONSUMER LOANS — INTEREST RATE METHODS ===

Cost-Plus Model: foundation for non-residential consumer loans

ANNUAL PERCENTAGE RATE (APR):
- Annualized internal rate of return that equates expected total payments with loan amount
- Takes into account how fast loan is repaid and how much credit customer has during life of loan
- Under Truth-in-Lending Act, lenders must give borrower an APR statement
- Allows borrowers to compare loan rates across lenders

APR in Hong Kong:
- Authorized Institutions (AIs) should use APRs for banking products to enable easier comparisons
- For products with commonly quoted floating rates (deposits, overdrafts, mortgages),
  APRs not mandatory but all related fees should be displayed
- AIs must address customer APR inquiries and share product APRs (per industry guidelines)
- AIs exempt from Money Lenders Ordinance (Cap. 163) interest rate restrictions
  (48% p.a. effective 30 December 2022)
- Should not charge extremely high interest; if APRs exceed lawful limits, AIs must justify
- Fees on defaulting loans must be reasonable

SIMPLE INTEREST RATE:
- Adjusts for length of time borrower actually has use of credit
- If customer paying off gradually, declining loan balance is used to determine interest

DISCOUNT RATE METHOD:
- Customer pays interest UP FRONT
- Interest deducted first; customer receives loan amount LESS interest owed

ADD-ON LOAN RATE METHOD:
- Oldest loan rate calculation method
- Interest added to principal before calculating installment payments
- Add-on rate equals simple interest rate ONLY if loan paid off in single lump sum at end


=== RULE OF 78s (Sum-Of-Digits Method) ===

Definition:
A rule of thumb to determine how much interest income a lender is entitled to accrue
at any point in time from a loan being paid in monthly installments.

Important when borrower wants to pay off a loan early.

Origin: sum of digits 1 through 12 = 78.

For 12-month loan: total interest divided into 78 portions
- Month 1 = 12/78 of interest
- Month 2 = 11/78
- Month 3 = 10/78
- ...
- Month 12 = 1/78

EXAMPLE (12-month loan, HK$12,000, monthly flat rate 0.296%, 1% handling fee p.a.):
- Each installment: HK$1,035.52
- Total Interest = Loan principal × monthly flat rate × tenor (months)
                 = HK$12,000 × 0.296% × 12 = HK$426.24
- 1st installment interest: HK$426.24 × 12/78 = HK$65.58
- 2nd installment interest: HK$426.24 × 11/78 = HK$60.11
- 3rd installment interest: HK$426.24 × 10/78 = HK$54.65
- ...
- Last installment interest: HK$426.24 × 1/78 = HK$5.46

Apportionment of principal/interest = total installment minus interest portion.

Early Repayment Variables:
- A: Sum of principal and interest payable for early full repayment
- L: Total loan amount
- I: Total interest for entire loan period if no early repayment
- R: Total principal and interest already repaid
- M: Number of remaining installments
- T: Total number of installments


=== INTEREST RATES ON HOME MORTGAGE LOANS ===

- Since 1930s: most loans were FIXED-RATE MORTGAGES (FRMs)
- 1981: Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs) authorized for federally chartered depository institutions
- ARMs created in response to inflation and volatile interest rate pressure
- Whether FRM or ARM, loan officer must determine initial loan rate and monthly payments


=== MORTGAGE POINTS ===

- Home mortgage agreements often require borrowers to pay an additional UP-FRONT charge called POINTS
- Points = prepaid interest; may be deductible as home mortgage interest
- Example: $100,000 home loan with 2 points up-front charge


=== MACRO-PRUDENTIAL POLICIES ===

Background:
- Pre-global financial crisis: policymakers used soft tools (communication, market discipline)
  to influence behavior and ensure stability
- Post-crisis: HARD policy measures required for systemic risks
- Strong macro-financial linkages mean systemic banking crises entail significant economic costs
- Economic activity reduces → deeper banking crisis → borrowers' financial condition deteriorates
- Risk of systemic crisis = SYSTEMIC RISK

Purposes:
1. Complement regulatory oversight of individual firms; build resilience (initially in banking)
2. Dampen volatility of financial cycle and reduce destabilizing imbalances


=== MACRO-PRUDENTIAL TOOLS ===

1. CAPITAL TOOLS:
   - Counter cyclical capital Buffer (CCB):
     • Increases resilience by building buffers when credit growth is high
     • May slow credit growth with higher cost of funds
     • Lowered during stressed periods
   - Other Systemically Important Institutions (O-SII) buffer:
     • Reduces probability of default of these institutions
   - Systemic risk buffer:
     • Prevents/mitigates long-term non-cyclical systemic risk not covered by other buffers
     • Can limit concentration of exposures and excessive leverage
   - Leverage Ratio (LR):
     • Limits leverage; backstop to risk-based capital buffers
     • Safeguards against errors in risk-based requirements
     • Research: LR outperformed risk-weighted ratios in predicting failures during crisis (IMF 2009)
   - Sectoral Capital Requirement (SCR):
     • Increases resilience by building buffers to losses in real estate
     • Targeted measure
     • Modest tightening in credit conditions (Bank of England 2014)

2. LIQUIDITY TOOLS:
   - Time-varying Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) / Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR):
     • Mitigates effects from market illiquidity, excessive maturity/liquidity mismatch
     • Builds on existing micro-prudential requirement
     • New Zealand introduced core funding ratio in 2010
   - Loan-to-Deposit Ratio (LDR):
     • Reduces reliance on short-term wholesale funding
     • Constrains credit growth during upturn
     • May contribute to deposit competition

3. CREDIT-RELATED TOOLS:
   - Loan-to-Value (LTV):
     • Reduces sensitivity to property price movements
     • Directly targeted at real estate
     • Directly affects credit cycle
   - Debt-to-Income (DTI):
     • Increases household/bank resilience through lower probability of default
     • More binding than LTVs (incomes grow slower than house prices)
     • IMF (2013): LTV/LTIs slow mortgage growth, reduce housing bubble potential, reduce downturn severity
     • Significant Asian experience with these measures

4. OTHER BALANCE SHEET TOOLS:
   - Exposure Limit:
     • Reduces systemic risk from concentration/interconnectedness
     • Reduces contagion risk
     • ECB (2013): contagion losses decline when 25% large exposure limit lowered
   - Disclosure:
     • Increases market discipline
     • Reduces market uncertainty and information contagion probability
     • US/EU stress tests improved market conditions
   - Pillar 2:
     • Bank's Pillar 2 requirement determined as part of Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP)
     • Flexible tool for wide range of systemic risks
     • Same economic impact as comparable macro-prudential instruments


=== LINKING TOOLS TO INTERMEDIATE OBJECTIVES ===

| Tools | Intermediate Objectives |
|-------|------------------------|
| Capital Tools (Cyclical/Structural Buffers) | Cyclical: Excessive credit growth and leverage |
| Liquidity Tools | Cyclical: Excessive maturity mismatch and market illiquidity |
| Credit-related Tools | Structural: Exposure concentration |
| Other Tools | Structural: Misaligned incentives |

(Source: IMF 2013, ESRB 2013)


=== INTERNATIONAL EXPERIENCE WITH MACRO-PRUDENTIAL POLICY ===

Hong Kong:
- LTV cap (since 1990s), differentiated by property and borrower type, adjusted counter-cyclically
- Used with mortgage insurance; DTI cap also used
- Risk addressed: high bank exposure to residential mortgages, lack of monetary policy control,
  high and volatile property price growth
- Effectiveness: bank losses remained low during Asian crisis after severe property value falls
  (BOE 2011); delinquency ratio of insured portfolio remains low

UK:
- Proportionate LTI cap at 4.5 times
- Risk addressed: insurance against unexpected housing market momentum
- Implemented in late 2014

Canada:
- 80% LTV cap with mandatory mortgage insurance
- Capped 25-year loan term
- Maximum total debt service ratio of 44%
- Time varying — Canada took four macro-prudential measures since 2008
- Risk addressed: domestic mortgage market, house price and credit growth
- IMF (2014a): moderation in house prices and mortgage credit since 2010
  partly due to policy measures; mortgage arrears rate in insurance portfolio remains low

Australia:
- Increase in risk weights for self-verified mortgages and non-prime home loans (2004)
- Risk addressed: strong house price growth with higher-risk lending
- Effect: helped change bank lending behavior and limit market growth

(Source: Grace, T., Hallissey, N., & Woods, M. (2015). The Instruments of macro-prudential policy.
Quarterly Bulletin, 1, 91-95.)


=== PRUDENTIAL MEASURES FOR MORTGAGE LOANS ===

Definition:
Regulatory measures by governments/financial authorities to ensure housing market and
financial system stability and manage mortgage lending risks.

Aims: prevent excessive borrowing, promote responsible lending, protect borrowers/lenders.

SINGAPORE:
- Loan-to-Value (LTV) Limits — set by MAS; stricter for second/subsequent purchases
- Total Debt Servicing Ratio (TDSR) — total monthly debt obligations not to exceed % of income
- Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty (ABSD) — tax on property purchases (foreign/multiple)
  to cool market and discourage speculation
- Interest Rate Floors — some banks ensure borrowers can service mortgages even when rates rise

HONG KONG:
- HKMA oversees prudential measures
- LTV and Debt Servicing Ratio Restrictions to limit excessive borrowing
- Stress Testing — banks conduct on borrowers to assess repayment ability under adverse conditions
  including rising rates


=== SME FINANCING GUARANTEE SCHEME (SFGS) ===

Background:
To address SME capital-flow challenges, the application period for the
80% and 90% Guarantee Products under SFGS extended for two years to end of March 2026.

SPECIAL 100% LOAN GUARANTEE:
- HKMC Insurance Limited (HKMCI), wholly-owned subsidiary of HK Mortgage Corporation Limited
- Special 100% Loan Guarantee under SFGS started receiving applications from 20 April 2020
- Anti-epidemic Fund enhanced this guarantee due to prolonged COVID-19 impact
- Applicable to all sectors; loans fully guaranteed by Government

Eligibility:
- Operating for at least 3 months as at end-December 2019
- Suffered at least 30% decline in sales turnover in any month since February 2020
  vs monthly average of any quarter in 2019

Terms:
- Interest rate: Prime Rate minus 2.5% per annum (current ~2.75%)
- All guarantee fees waived
- Maximum loan per enterprise: total of employee wages and rents for 6 months OR HK$2 million,
  whichever is LOWER
- Optional principal moratorium for first 6 months to alleviate immediate repayment burden


=== EFFECTIVENESS OF SME FINANCING GUARANTEE ===

Impact of SFGS on Loans and Creditworthiness:
- Significant impact on loan amounts
- Other key determinants: collateral arrangements, reporting bank, economic sector, loan purpose
- Varying credit uplift across industries — particularly beneficial for vulnerable sectors
  or loans for uncertain purposes during pandemic
- Enhanced credit access even for borrowers with significant collateral
- Significantly REDUCES probability of loan downgrades by 6 percentage points
- Provides evidence of stabilizing effect on perceived borrower creditworthiness
