=== INTRODUCTION ===
- Many issues such as credit risk and the burden of raising new capital to meet customer
  funding needs and regulatory standards keep managers busy
- New tools (securitizing loans, selling loans off balance sheet, standby letters of credit,
  credit derivatives) help with risk management
  - Control risk more effectively
  - Open new sources of fee income
- The 2007-2009 credit crisis showed these tools have significant limitations:
  unexpected risks, extreme complexity that can wreak havoc on financial systems


=== SECURITIZING LOANS AND OTHER ASSETS ===

Definition: A simple idea for raising new funds where a lending institution sets aside
a group of income-earning, relatively illiquid assets (home mortgages, credit card loans)
and sells relatively liquid securities (financial claims) against those assets in the open market.
Effectively: loans transformed into publicly traded securities.

Key Players:
- Originator: the lender whose loans are securitized
- Issuer: a Special-Purpose Entity (SPE) — separated from originator so that bankruptcy
  of originator doesn't affect credit status of pooled loans ("bankruptcy remote")
- Credit Rating Agency: rates securities so investors know what new instruments are worth
  (potential moral hazard problem)
- Security Underwriter (Investment Banker): sells securities in money/capital markets
- Trustee: ensures issuer fulfills transfer requirements and provides promised services
- Servicer (often the originator): collects payments and passes them to trustee,
  who then ensures investors receive timely payments
- Credit Enhancer: provides guarantees against default
- Liquidity Enhancer


=== HISTORY OF SECURITIZATION ===
Concept began in the US residential mortgage market.

Three Government-Sponsored Enterprises (GSEs) improved salability of mortgage loans:
- GNMA (Ginnie Mae) — Government National Mortgage Association
- FNMA (Fannie Mae) — Federal National Mortgage Association
- FHLMC (Freddie Mac) — Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation

Note: Long-range outlook for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac is questionable due to recent
record defaults on home loans they traded.


=== COLLATERALIZED MORTGAGE OBLIGATIONS (CMOs) ===
Developed in the 1980s by Freddie Mac with First Boston Corporation (later Credit Suisse).

Multi-step process:
1. Home mortgages first pooled together
2. GNMA-guaranteed securities issued against the loan pool
3. Securities offered to investors globally
4. Securities placed in a trust account off the lender's balance sheet
5. Different classes of CMOs (tranches) issued as claims against the security pool

Tranches:
- Each tranche promises a different rate of return (coupon) and carries different risk exposure
- Interest payments: each tranche receives entitled payments
- Principal payments flow first to senior tranche until fully retired,
  then to next tranche, and so on down the "waterfall" until last tranche is paid out
- "Senior" tranches generally have shorter maturities — reduce reinvestment risk,
  attractive to risk-averse investors


=== TYPES OF SECURITIZED ASSETS ===
- Residential Mortgages (the beginnings)
- Riskier CMOs
- Home Equity Loans
- Automobile Loans
- Commercial Mortgages
- Small Business Administration Loans
- Mobile Home Loans
- Credit Card Receivables
- Truck Leases
- Computer Leases


=== ADVANTAGES OF SECURITIZATION ===
1. Diversifies a bank's credit risk exposure
2. Creates liquid assets out of illiquid assets
3. Transforms these assets into new sources of capital
4. Allows the bank to hold a more geographically diversified loan portfolio
5. Allows the bank to better manage interest rate risk
6. Allows the bank to generate fee income


=== REGULATORY CONCERNS POST 2007-2009 CRISIS ===
Regulators looking closely at:
1. Risk of having to come up with large amounts of liquidity in a hurry
   (to pay investors holding asset-backed securities and cover bad loans)
2. Risk of agreeing to underwrite asset-backed securities that cannot be sold
3. Risk of acting as credit enhancer and underestimating loan-loss reserves
4. Risk that unqualified trustees fail to protect investors
5. Risk of loan servicers being unable to monitor loan performance and collect monies


=== SALES OF LOANS TO RAISE FUNDS AND REDUCE RISK ===
- Carried out by financial firms of widely varying sizes
- Leading sellers: Deutsche Bank, JP Morgan Chase, Bank of America, ING Bank (Netherlands)
- Only a minority of US depository institutions report regular and significant asset sales
- Concentrated among residential mortgage credits and miscellaneous household loans
- Most loans sold mature within 90 days; may be new or seasoned

Mechanics:
- Seller usually retains servicing rights — generates fee income
- Servicer collects interest/principal from borrowers, passes proceeds to buyers
- Servicer monitors borrower performance and adherence to loan terms
- Most loans purchased in million-dollar units by investors with special debtor knowledge


=== TYPES OF LOAN SALES ===
1. PARTICIPATION LOANS
   - Outside party purchases a loan
   - Generally no influence over loan terms
2. ASSIGNMENTS
   - Ownership of loan transferred to buyer
   - Buyer has direct claim against borrower
3. LOAN STRIP
   - Short-dated pieces of longer-term loans, maturing in days/weeks

Most popular: Participation loans and assignments


=== BUYERS OF LOAN SALES ===
- Often segmented (e.g., distressed HLT loan buyers = vulture funds, investment banks, hedge funds)
- Vulture Funds: specialized hedge funds investing in distressed loans

Vulture Fund's Cycle of Profits:
1. Poor country defaults on debt held by creditor country
2. Vulture fund buys defaulted debt from creditor country for pennies on the dollar
3. Vulture funds sue poor country for full amount of debt + interest + penalties
4. Vulture funds recoup massive profits off defaulted debt from poor country

Other major buyers:
- Foreign banks (important buyer of domestic loans)
- Insurance companies and pension funds (long-term loans)
- Open- and Closed-End Bank Loan Mutual funds (e.g., Highland Capital Management of Dallas)
- Nonfinancial corporations (e.g., GE Capital, AIG)


=== SELLERS OF LOAN SALES ===
- Major money center banks, US government, government agencies
- Good Bank — Bad Bank Structure:
  - Subsidiary banks specializing in handling Non-Performing Loans (NPLs)
  - Increases value of Good Bank
  - Allows structuring of Bad Bank to improve management incentives and operating efficiency
  - Proposed to remove toxic assets in the crisis
- Foreign banks (e.g., Japanese banks in California)
- Investment Banks (Merrill Lynch, generally large HLTs)
- US Government & Agencies (e.g., HUD)
  - Increased due to Federal Debt Collection Improvements Act, 1996
  - 2007-2011 housing crisis left ~820,000 severely delinquent mortgages with HUD,
    Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac (and US taxpayers)


=== REASONS BEHIND LOAN SALES ===
1. Rid bank of lower-yielding assets to make room for higher-yielding assets when rates rise
2. Increase marketability and liquidity of assets
3. Eliminate credit and interest rate risk
4. Generate fee income
5. Purchasing bank diversifies loan portfolio and reduces risk


=== RISKS IN LOAN SALES ===
1. Best quality loans easiest to sell — may increase earnings volatility for selling bank
2. Loans purchased from another bank can turn bad just as easily as own loans
3. Loan sales are cyclical


=== FACTORS AFFECTING LOAN SALES GROWTH ===
- Access to commercial paper market
  - Since 1987, large banks can underwrite commercial paper
  - More smaller middle-market firms gaining direct access
- Customer relationship effects (customers may dislike having loan sold to another party)
- Legal concerns (fraudulent conveyance proceedings)


=== FACTORS ENCOURAGING FUTURE LOAN SALES GROWTH ===
- Bank for International Settlement (BIS) capital requirements
- Market value accounting (marking to market makes loans look more like securities)
- Asset brokerage and loan trading
- Government loan sales
- Credit rating of loans offered for sale
- Purchase and sale of foreign bank loans (e.g., Goldman Sachs fund for Japan's SMFG)


=== BANKING REFORM IN CHINA — NPL LOAN SALES ===

1998-1999: The Big Four state-owned banks (SOBs) replenished capital and cleaned up
balance sheets through capital injection, NPA stripping, financial restructuring.

Four Financial Asset Management Companies (金融资产管理公司) established by
the Ministry of Finance — one as a "bad bank" for each Big Four:
- China Great Wall Asset Management (中国长城资产管理公司) — for ABC
- China Orient Asset Management (中国东方资产管理公司) — for BOC
- China Huarong Asset Management (中国华融资产管理股份有限公司) — for ICBC
- China Cinda Asset Management (中国信达资产管理股份有限公司) — for CCB

Results:
- Four AMCs separated RMB 1,394 billion of NPLs from Big Four Banks and CDB
- Lowered NPL ratio of Big Four Banks by an average of 10%
- Since 2001, China gradually implemented five-category asset classification
- Gradually lowered business tax of commercial banks
- First round mainly technical (NPL handling, internal management) without
  addressing in-depth systemic issues


=== BAD BANKS WITH CHINESE CHARACTERISTICS — ONGOING TRANSFORMATION ===

Asset Management Companies (AMCs):
- "Bad banks" — important policy tool for systemic NPL problems
- Establishment often associated with large recapitalization, bank resolutions, mergers
- Aim to stabilize distressed banks and repair credit channel
- Significant fiscal costs

Recent developments:
- Local AMCs set up since 2013 — addressing NPL issues at provincial level
- 2019-2020: Chinese government repeatedly intervened to rescue ailing banks
- 17 December 2022: CBIRC approved China Galaxy Asset Management Co., Ltd.
  (中国银河资产管理有限责任公司) (CGAM) to commence operations

Big Four SOBs:
- ICBC, CCB, ABC, BOC accounted for 70% of total bank assets at the time
- Each AMC was 'paired' with one Big Four bank
- Banks initially specialized in: credit/savings, infrastructure investment,
  agricultural finance, foreign exchange — determining initial AMC asset structure

Debt-to-Equity Swap (DES) Mechanism:
- Losses transferred from SOBs to AMCs
- AMCs given stakes (sometimes controlling) in distressed SOBs
- Major departure from traditional AMC purpose
- Banks transferred bad assets at face value (without loss)
- SOBs replaced part of bank debt obligations with equity held by AMCs


=== KEY REASONS FOR SELLING/LIQUIDATING BAD LOANS THROUGH BAD BANK ===
1. Use of workout specialists in managing the liquidation
2. Improvement in good bank's reputation after removing bad assets — better access to deposit/funding markets
3. Ability to dispose of bad assets without liquidity concerns (lack of deposits)
4. Customize incentive agreements for managers to generate enhanced loan-sale values
5. Increased informational symmetry about good bank's asset value

=== INTRODUCTION ===
- Even taking on risk, financial institution must protect net worth from erosion
- Managers look at asset and liability portfolios as integrated whole
- Asset-Liability Management (ALM) protects against business cycles and seasonal pressures


=== ASSET-LIABILITY MANAGEMENT (ALM) ===
A financial technique to manage mismatch of asset/liability and/or cash flow risks.

Key concepts:
- Mismatched risks: different underlying factors causing assets/liabilities to move differently
- Cash flow matching (immunization): match asset and liability cash flows exactly
  so any yield curve movement is irrelevant
- Duration matching: manage mismatch when no fixed cash flows

Asset-Liability Risk = Leveraged Form of Risk:
- Small changes in assets/liabilities can significantly impact capital due to leverage
- Banks, insurance companies, pension funds use these techniques to manage mismatched
  asset/liability risks (especially interest rate) and ensure capital not depleted

Examples of embedded options:
- Insurance policies: Guaranteed rates, cash surrender values, policy loans, dividends/bonuses
- Banks: Prepayment option to borrowers, overdraft, early withdrawal
- Pension plans: Early retirement, cash-out option, defined contribution conversion
- Assets: Callable options, prepayment options, abandon option (credit/bankruptcy)


=== ALM TECHNIQUES AND BROADER APPLICATIONS ===

Risk Assessment Techniques:
- Gap Analysis
- Duration Analysis
- Monte Carlo Simulation (for complex markets)

Other Strategies:
- Use of OTC derivatives for hedging
- Securitization techniques to remove assets/liabilities from balance sheet

Broader Scope:
- Now applied in nonfinancial industries to manage interest-rate, commodity price,
  liquidity, and foreign exchange risks


=== ASSET vs LIABILITY STRATEGIES ===

ASSET MANAGEMENT STRATEGY (control over assets, no control over liabilities):
- Asset allocation
- Structured products (derivatives, swaps, credit options, interest rate options)
- Alpha & Beta approach to asset allocation
- Timing

LIABILITY MANAGEMENT STRATEGY (control liabilities by changing rates and terms):
- Pricing margin/reserves
- Reinsurance / Securitization
- Acquisition/sale of business lines
- Redesigning/reengineering products
- Timing


=== SIX STEPS TO IMPLEMENT EFFECTIVE ALM ===
1. SETTING ALM OBJECTIVES
   - Decide objectives based on stakeholders, regulators, competition, ratings
   - Minimize volatility of surpluses? Higher yield? Maximum risk level?

2. RISK FACTORS AND CASH FLOW STRUCTURE
   - Capture various risks and complex interactions between asset/liability cash flows
   - Risk factors: market, interest rate, credit risks, contractual obligations like options

3. AVAILABLE HEDGING SOLUTIONS
   - Diversification reduces non-systematic risks
   - Firm-specific risks cannot be diversified easily
   - Evaluate types of assets, hedging instruments (derivatives, options, pricing, reinsurance),
     capital market instruments (securitization, M&A)

4. MODELING RISK FACTORS
   - For interest rate risks, modeling yield curve is critical

5. SETTING DECISION VARIABLES
   - Insurer: asset allocation, dividends/bonuses, new business amounts, pricing
   - Decision variables differ for various FIs

6. SETTING CONSTRAINTS
   - Heavy regulation (reserve and capital requirements)
   - Accounting requirements (or profits) increasingly important
   - Constraints modeled to ensure regulatory and accounting compliance


=== ASSET AND LIABILITY COMMITTEE (ALCO) ===

- Important feature in effective management of Authorized Institution's (AI) assets and liabilities
- Most fundamental function: oversee operations relating to interest rate risk and liquidity risk
- Ensures AI has adequate funds to meet obligations
- Other functions depend on AI's size and asset/liability mix
- Balanced representation needed for both balance sheet sides

Composition (senior staff):
- Chief Executive
- Chief Financial Officer
- Treasurer
- Chief Credit Officer
- Officer in charge of deposit-taking
- (Larger banks) Division heads of corporate and retail banking

Source: HKMA Supervisory Policy Manual (CG-1): Corporate Governance of Locally Incorporated AIs


=== INTEREST RATE RISK — ONE OF THE GREATEST MANAGEMENT CHALLENGES ===

Changing rates impact both balance sheet and income statement.

Two key risks:
- PRICE RISK: When rates rise, market value of bond/asset falls
- REINVESTMENT RISK: When rates fall, coupon payments reinvested at lower rates

Forces Determining Interest Rates:
- Loanable Funds Theory

Measurement of Interest Rates:
- Yield to Maturity (YTM)
- Bank Discount Rate

Components of Interest Rates:
- Risk-free real rate of interest
- Various risk premiums:
  - Default Risk
  - Inflation Risk
  - Liquidity Risk
  - Call Risk
  - Maturity Risk

Interest rates = "the price of credit"
- Demanded by lenders as compensation for use of borrowed funds
- Expressed in percentage points and basis points (1/100 of a percentage point)


=== YIELD TO MATURITY (YTM) ===
The discount rate that equalizes current market value of a loan/security
with the expected stream of future income payments it will generate.

Assumes:
- 365-day year
- Interest income is compounded at the calculated YTM


=== BANK DISCOUNT RATE (DR) ===
Often quoted on short-term loans and money market securities (e.g., Treasury bills).

Characteristics:
- Ignores compounding effect
- Based on a 360-day year
- Uses face value of instrument to calculate yield/return
  → makes calculations easier but theoretically incorrect
- Purchase price is a much better base for calculating true return

Formula:
  Bank Discount Rate = [(Face Value − Purchase Price) / Face Value] × (360 / Days to Maturity)


=== YIELD CURVES ===
- Graphical picture of relationship between yields and maturities of securities
- Generally created with treasury securities to keep default risk constant

Shapes:
- UPWARD: long-term rates higher than short-term rates
- DOWNWARD: short-term rates higher than long-term rates
- HORIZONTAL: short-term and long-term rates equal

Implications for lending institutions:
- Most banks experience POSITIVE MATURITY GAP (avg asset maturity > avg liability maturity)
- Upward-sloping curve → revenues from longer-term assets > expenses from shorter-term liabilities
  → positive Net Interest Margin
- Flat or negatively sloped curve → small or negative NIM


=== PROTECT THE NET INTEREST MARGIN (NIM) — GOAL OF HEDGING ===

Most popular hedging strategy: INTEREST-SENSITIVE GAP MANAGEMENT

Gap management requires analysis of maturities and repricing opportunities of:
- Interest-bearing assets
- Interest-bearing liabilities

If excessively exposed to rate risk, match volume of repriceable assets with
volume of repriceable liabilities during same time period.


=== KEY GAP MEASURES ===

DOLLAR IS GAP:
  Dollar IS GAP = Interest-Sensitive Assets (ISA) − Interest-Sensitive Liabilities (ISL)
- Positive Dollar IS GAP → asset sensitive
- Negative Dollar IS GAP → liability sensitive
Example: ISA = $150M, ISL = $200M → Dollar IS GAP = −$50M (liability sensitive)

RELATIVE IS GAP:
  Relative IS GAP = (ISA − ISL) / Total Assets
- Greater than zero → asset sensitive
- Negative → liability sensitive

INTEREST SENSITIVITY RATIO (ISR):
  ISR = ISA / ISL
- ISR < 1 → liability-sensitive
- ISR > 1 → asset-sensitive
- ISR = 1 → relatively insulated from interest rate risk

POSITIONS:
- Asset Sensitive: ISA > ISL (positive gap)
- Liability Sensitive: ISL > ISA (negative gap)


=== KEY DECISIONS IN GAP MANAGEMENT ===
1. Choose time period during which NIM is to be managed and length of subperiods ("maturity buckets")
2. Choose target level for net interest margin
3. To increase NIM: develop correct rate forecast OR reallocate earning assets/liabilities
   to increase spread between revenues and expenses
4. Determine volume of interest-sensitive assets and liabilities to hold


=== FACTORS INFLUENCING NET INTEREST MARGIN ===
1. Changes in level of interest rates
2. Changes in spread between asset yields and liability costs
3. Changes in volume of interest-bearing (earning) assets — expansion/contraction
4. Changes in volume of interest-bearing liabilities used to fund earning assets
5. Changes in mix of assets and liabilities — floating vs fixed-rate, shorter vs longer maturity,
   higher vs lower expected yields


=== CUMULATIVE GAP ===
- Total dollar difference between assets and liabilities that can be repriced over a designated period
- Useful overall measure of interest rate risk exposure
- Allows approximate calculation of how net interest income changes with rate changes


=== PROBLEMS WITH INTEREST-SENSITIVE GAP MANAGEMENT ===
- Interest paid on liabilities tends to move faster than rates earned on assets
- Rates on assets/liabilities don't move at same speed as market rates
- Repricing point of some assets/liabilities not easy to identify
- Does NOT consider impact of changing rates on equity positions


=== DURATION AS A RISK-MANAGEMENT TOOL ===

Definition:
A value-weighted and time-weighted measure of maturity considering the timing of
all cash inflows from earning assets and all cash outflows associated with liabilities.
Measures the average maturity of a promised stream of future cash payments.

Net Worth (NW) Concept:
- NW = Value of Assets − Value of Liabilities
- As market rates change, asset/liability values change → net worth changes


=== PORTFOLIO THEORY INSIGHTS ===
1. A rise in market rates causes market value (price) of fixed-rate assets and liabilities to decline
2. Longer maturity → larger decline in market value when rates rise

By equating asset and liability durations:
- Balance average maturity of expected cash inflows (assets) with cash outflows (liabilities)
- Stabilize / IMMUNIZE the market value of net worth


=== KEY DURATION INSIGHT ===
Duration measures sensitivity of market value of financial instruments to interest rate changes.

Percentage change in market price = Duration × Relative change in interest rates (for that asset/liability)


=== CONVEXITY ===
Relationship between asset's price change and yield/rate change.

Definition: presence of a NONLINEAR relationship between changes in asset's price and changes in market rates.

Properties:
- A number to aid portfolio managers in measuring/controlling market risk
- An asset/portfolio with low duration AND low convexity → relatively small market risk
- Convexity INCREASES with duration of an asset
- Tells us: rate of change in asset's price for a given rate change varies with prevailing rate level
