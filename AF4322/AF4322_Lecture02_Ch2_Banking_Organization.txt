=== TYPES OF BANKS ===

1. MONEY CENTER BANKS
   Definition: Large banks in major financial centers (NY, Chicago, San Francisco)
   Examples: JPMorgan Chase ($3.7T assets), Bank of America ($3.1T), Citigroup ($2.4T)
   Characteristics:
   - Vast balance sheets, trillions in assets
   - Complex, diversified operations
   - Significant influence on global financial system
   - Heavy involvement in capital markets, derivatives trading, M&A
   - Often "too big to fail" — strictest regulatory oversight

2. REGIONAL BANKS (US)
   Definition: Large banks serving multiple states but not nationwide; significant presence in particular region
   Examples: U.S. Bancorp ($600B), Truist Financial ($535B), PNC Financial ($600B)
   Characteristics:
   - Hundreds of billions in assets, multi-state operations, mix of retail and commercial
   - Strong regional market share
   - Some investment banking services, regional market expertise
   - Important role in regional economies

3. COMMUNITY BANKS
   Definition: Smaller banks serving local communities
   Examples: First National Bank of Smallville — $2B assets, 25 branches in 3 counties
   - Specialize in agricultural and small business lending
   - Declining: 14,000 banks (1980) → 4,700 banks (2024)
   Characteristics:
   - $100M-$10B in assets, 1-50 branches, relationship banking
   - Deep understanding of local market conditions
   - Strong customer relationships, personalized service
   Key competitive advantages:
   - Local decision-making
   - Flexibility in lending
   - Relationship-based banking


=== THE DUAL BANKING SYSTEM IN THE US ===
- Banks chartered at federal OR state level
- Federal charter: Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)
- State charter: State banking agency
- Creates dynamic regulatory environment
- Banks choose charter based on business model and strategic objectives
- Regulatory arbitrage: banks may switch charters to optimize regulation


=== ORGANIZATIONAL STRUCTURES ===

--- UNIT BANKS ---
Definition: Bank operates from one location only
Historical: Most common before 1970s, now <5% of US banks
Advantages:
- Simple to manage, clear decision-making, low overhead, local focus
Disadvantages:
- Cannot achieve economies of scale, vulnerable to local downturns
Why disappeared: Technology costs too high, customers want multiple branches


--- BRANCHING ---
Definition: Bank operates multiple branches under one charter
Branch = separate banking office offering deposit and lending services

US Historical Evolution (branches per bank):
- 1934: 0.21
- 1970: 5.2
- 2010: 12.48 (peak, after Riegle-Neal 1994)
- 2024: 10.2 (digital banking reducing branch need)

How it works: Single charter, multiple locations, centralized management
Advantages: Economies of scale, wider geographic reach, better diversification
Example: Bank of America — 3,800 branches nationwide, serves 70+ million customers (2023 Annual Report)

Branching Organizations:
- Unit firm grows → establishes branching organization
- Full range of services from several locations: head office + full-service branches
- Limited services through:
  • Drive-in windows
  • ATMs
  • Networked computers
  • Point-of-sale (POS) terminals
  • Internet
  • Other communications systems
- Senior management at home office; branches have own management with limited authority

Branching Expansion History:
- 1930s Great Depression: only 1 in 5 American banks had a branch office
- Early 21st century: average US bank had ~12 full-service branches
- Riegle-Neal Interstate Banking and Branching Efficiency Act (1994) enabled expansion
- Population exodus to suburbs contributed
- Recent years: branch expansion has slowed

--- FINANCIAL HOLDING COMPANY (FHC) ---
Definition: A US holding company that can own banks AND non-banking financial companies

Legal basis: Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (1999) = Financial Services Modernization Act
- Repealed parts of Glass-Steagall Act
- Allowed affiliations among banks, securities firms, insurance companies under one holding structure

Requirements to become FHC:
1. Bank subsidiaries must be well-capitalized
2. Bank subsidiaries must be well-managed
3. Satisfactory CRA rating

Subsidiaries can include:
Banks, insurance companies, investment banks, securities firms, mutual funds, asset management

- Integrated model captures synergies and cross-selling opportunities
- Complexity creates management and regulatory challenges
Example: JPMorgan Chase owns JPMorgan Bank, JPMorgan Securities, JPMorgan Asset Management, Chase Insurance (not a major insurance subsidiary)

FHC Advantages vs Disadvantages:
| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|---------------|
| 1. Activity & Geographic Freedom: broad services, national/regional reach | 1. Community Harm: drains capital from local markets, harms small businesses |
| 2. Financial Synergies: economies of scope (cross-selling) and scale; efficient capital allocation | 2. Systemic Risk: creates "too big to fail" institutions threatening financial stability |
| 3. Risk Management: diversified revenue, lower bank failure rates | 3. Complexity & Conflicts: regulatory arbitrage, bureaucratic inefficiency, conflicts of interest |

In essence: FHC is the primary vehicle for creating large, complex financial institutions dominating the US economy. Diversification/scope advantages come bundled with trade-offs in community impact, systemic risk, and regulatory challenge.


--- GLOBAL COMPARISON: UNIVERSAL BANKING HOLDING COMPANIES ---
In many countries, financial holding companies / universal banks own both banking and non-banking subsidiaries.

European Union:
- EU Banking Directives + Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID) allow universal banking
- Reforms in 1980s/1990s enabled banks to own insurance, securities, asset management firms
- Example: BNP Paribas (France)

United Kingdom:
- Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA) allows financial conglomerates across banking, insurance, investment
- Example: HSBC Holdings plc (UK)


=== INTERNAL ORGANIZATION OF THE BANKING FIRM ===

Differences in size lead to marked differences in internal organization and services offered.

How is a bank organized internally to achieve its goals?
- Includes management structure, decision-making, functional divisions
- Reflects bank's strategy and competitive positioning
- Determines operating efficiency
Key insight: Different organizational structures lead to different competitive strategies


--- COMMUNITY BANKS / COMMUNITY-ORIENTED FIRMS ---
- Devoted to smaller, locally-based deposits and loans (a.k.a. retail banks)
- Sharp contrast to wholesale banks
- Close contact between top management, division management, staff
- Significantly impacted by local economy and new regulations
- Losing ground in numbers and industry shares

Community Bank Decline:
- 1985: ~14,000 (Fed Reserve Bank of St. Louis 2013)
- 2010: ~6,000 (Fed Reserve Bank of St. Louis 2013)
- 2023: 4,001 (FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile Q4 2023)

Smaller Community Bank Org Chart features:
- Structure: Simple, flat hierarchy
- Management: CEO, CFO, Chief Lending Officer, branch managers; close mgmt-staff contact
- Decision-Making: Centralized, quick, responsive to local needs
- Strategy: Relationship-based, local market focus, personalized service
- Strengths: Flexibility, local knowledge, strong customer relationships
- Weakness: Limited specialization

Typical divisions: Lending Division (Commercial Loan Officers, Consumer Loan Officers, Compliance), Accounting and Operations, Fund-Raising and Marketing (Tellers, New Accounts, Advertising), Trust Division (Personal Trusts, Business Trusts, Safekeeping)


--- LARGER BANKS (Money Center, Wholesale, Retail) ---
- Located in large city, focus on wholesale or wholesale + retail
- Many use profit-centered / performance approach
- Each major dept maximizes contribution to profitability or another performance indicator

Advantages over community-oriented institutions:
- Global reach
- Better diversified — geographically and by product line
- Withstand fluctuating economy better
- Raise huge financial capital at low cost
- Attract top managerial talent

Org Structure:
- Board of Directors
- CEO / Executive Management
- Major divisions:
  • Retail Banking
  • Commercial Banking
  • Investment Banking
  • Wealth Management
  • Risk Management
  • Technology/Operations
- Global operations with regional management

Money Center / Wholesale Bank Org Chart features:
- Administrative Services Group (Senior Executive Committee, Board Chairman, President/CEO, SVPs)
- Fund Raising and Funds Management Division (Investments and Funding Group, Portfolio Mgmt and Money-Market Desk, Institutional Banking Dept, Capital Markets Dept, Asset/Liability Mgmt, Planning and Risk Control)
- International Banking Dept (Global Finance Foreign Offices Group)
- Funds-Allocation Division (Commercial Financial Services Group, Commercial Credit, Commercial Real Estate, Corporate Services, Credit and Debit Cards, Loan Review Group, Loan Workout Group)
- Operations Department (Auditing and Control, Branch Office Mgmt, Computer Systems, HR, Teller Dept, Securities Dept, Regulatory Compliance Group)
- Personal Financial Services Division (Private Banking Services, Trust Services, Residential Lending, Customer Services, Safe Deposit, Motor/ATM/Electronic Banking, Marketing)

=== BANK MERGERS & ACQUISITIONS ===

Reshaping the Financial-Services Sector:
- Branching, BHCs, and FHCs growth fueled by multiple factors
- M&A is another powerful force
- Bigger companies pursue smaller financial-service providers, buy assets in great numbers
- Since 1980: more than 12,000 bank mergers in the US

Advantages of M&A:
1. Economies of Scale: spread fixed costs (IT, compliance) over more customers
2. Economies of Scope: cross-sell services, increase customer lifetime value
3. Geographic Expansion: enter new markets, increase market share, diversify risk
4. Acquire Expertise: gain skilled management, customer relationships, technology
5. Regulatory Requirements: forced mergers to prevent failures during crises


=== EFFICIENCY AND SIZE OF FINANCIAL FIRMS ===

Do Bigger Financial Firms Operate at Lower Cost?
- Two possible sources of cost savings:
  • Economies of scale
  • Economies of scope
- Evidence: at least moderate economies of scale in banking
- Most studies find weak/no evidence for economies of scope
- Nonbank financial firms show similar conclusions

ECONOMIES OF SCALE:
Definition: Average cost of production falls as output increases
Formula: Average Cost = Total Cost / Number of Units
- Fixed Costs: IT systems, compliance infrastructure, executive management, cybersecurity
- Variable Costs: Teller salaries, customer service, marketing, deposit insurance
Key insight: Larger banks spread fixed costs over more customers, reducing per-unit cost

Examples:

(a) Marketing/Customer Acquisition (National TV ad campaign cost $50M):
- Small Bank ($2B assets, 100K customers): $50M / 100K = $500/customer — prohibitive
- Large Bank ($200B assets, 50M customers): $50M / 50M = $1/customer — affordable
- Real example: BofA Super Bowl ads ($7M for 30 seconds) vs community bank local ads

(b) IT Infrastructure (new core banking system = $1B investment):
- Bank A ($10B assets): 0.01% of assets — huge burden, 1% of profits
- Bank B ($100B assets): 0.001% of assets — manageable
- Bank C ($1T assets): 0.0001% of assets — barely noticeable

(c) Regulatory Compliance:
- Small Bank ($2B assets): 15 staff × $100K = $1.5M/yr (0.075% of assets)
- Large Bank ($200B assets): 500 staff × $100K = $50M/yr (0.025% of assets)
- Result: Large bank's compliance cost is 1/3 per dollar of assets
- Why: Regulatory requirements largely fixed regardless of size
- Impact: Small banks pay 3x more per dollar of assets — significant disadvantage

MOST EFFICIENT SIZES (U-shaped cost curve):
- Costs fall as size increases (economies of scale)
- Costs may rise at very large sizes (diseconomies)
- Optimal size varies by business model and market
- Estimated lowest-cost bank size range: starts around $200M assets, extends through billions


=== FINANCIAL FIRM GOALS: IMPACT ON COST, EFFICIENCY, PERFORMANCE ===

EXPENSE-PREFERENCE BEHAVIOR:
- When mgmt prioritizes manager benefits over stockholders or public

=== GOALS OF FINANCIAL FIRM ===
- Facilities established for customer convenience
- Historically convenience meant location

Three main options to respond to changing demands:
1. Chartering new (de novo) financial institutions
2. Establishing new full-service branch offices
3. Setting up limited-service facilities


=== CHARTERING A NEW (DE NOVO) FINANCIAL-SERVICE INSTITUTION ===

Authority:
- Federal charter: Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)
- State charter: State banking commission

Federal charter characteristics:
- More rigorous standards
- Greater prestige
- Uniform national regulation

State charter characteristics:
- May be easier to obtain
- Lower costs
- More flexibility in some areas

Why government scrutiny is heightened:
1. They hold the public's savings
2. They are at the heart of the payments process — failure could disrupt commerce
3. They have ability to create money (through credit) — too many charters could destabilize


--- BANK CHARTERING PROCESS IN THE US ---
- Only state banking commissions (50 states) and OCC can issue charters of incorporation
- Federal standards generally more rigorous than state rules
- Organizers often seek federal charter for added prestige (especially for large depositors)
- Choice between federal or state = weighing benefits and costs for that bank

Benefits of Federal (National) Charter:
- Added prestige due to stricter regulatory standards (may attract larger deposits)
- Higher quality technical assistance from national authorities in trouble (better survival)
- Federal rules can pre-empt state laws

Benefits of State Charter:
- Easier and less costly to secure; lower supervisory fees
- Need not join Federal Reserve System
- Some states allow higher % of capital lent to single borrower
- May offer some services national banks cannot


--- QUESTIONS REGULATORS ASK NEW (DE NOVO) BANK ORGANIZERS ---
1. Population and geographic boundaries of the Primary Service Area (PSA)?
2. Number of competing banks/credit unions/finance companies in PSA — services, hours, distances?
3. Number, types, sizes of businesses in the area?
4. Traffic patterns, road adequacy, geographic barriers?
5. Population growth, incomes, occupations, education, age distribution in proposed PSA?
6. Financial history of the community, frequency/track record of new financial firms there?
7. Who owns stock issued? Amount held by organizers, directors, officers?
8. How experienced are organizers, management, board of directors?
9. Projections for deposits, loans, revenues, operating expenses, net income for first few years?


--- FACTORS WEIGHING ON THE DECISION TO SEEK A NEW CHARTER ---

External factors:
a. Level and growth of economic activity
b. Need for a new financial firm
c. Strength and character of competition

Internal factors:
a. Qualifications and contacts of organizers
b. Management quality
c. Pledging capital to cover charter application costs and startup


=== ESTABLISHING FULL-SERVICE BRANCH OFFICES ===

When/Why:
- Established institution wants to enter new markets, OR valued customers move
- Branches usually MUCH cheaper than chartering new firms
- US branching leader: Bank of America (>6,000 US offices)
- Location, design, services depend on customer + management/employee preferences

Desirable site characteristics for full-service branches:
1. Heavy traffic count
2. Large numbers of retail stores
3. Above-average age population
4. Substantial business owners, managers, professionals nearby
5. Steady or declining number of competitor facilities
6. Above-average population growth
7. Above-average population density
8. Relatively high target ratio of population per branch
9. Above-average household income


--- TARGET RATIO FORMULA (Eq. 4-1) ---
Population per branch office = Total population in area to be served / Number of branch offices in area

Larger population per office → more services purchased → more revenue, better operating efficiency


--- BRANCH AS CAPITAL BUDGETING DECISION ---

Cash outflow to fund a new branch (Eq. 4-2):
Cash outflow = NCF₁/(1+E(r))¹ + NCF₂/(1+E(r))² + ... + NCFₙ/(1+E(r))ⁿ

where:
- NCFₜ = Net Cash Flow in period t
- E(r) = expected return / discount rate
- n = number of periods

Other considerations:
a. Variance around expected return (mainly local economic conditions)
b. Covariance of expected returns from new branch, existing branches, and other assets


--- IMPACT ON INSTITUTION TOTAL RETURN (Eq. 4-3) ---
E(R_T) = W × E(R_B) + (1 − W) × E(R_OA)

where:
- R_T = total return
- R_B = expected return from new branch
- R_OA = expected return from other assets
- W = proportion of total resources invested in new branch B
- (1 − W) = proportion in all other assets


--- IMPACT ON OVERALL RISK (VARIANCE OF TOTAL RETURN) (Eq. 4-4) ---
σ²(R_T) = W²σ²(R_B) + (1 − W)²σ²(R_OA) + 2W(1 − W)·COV(R_B, R_OA)

Covariance (Eq. 4-5):
COV(R_B, R_OA) = ρ_B,OA × σ_B × σ_OA

where:
- ρ_B,OA = correlation coefficient between new branch return and other-asset returns
- σ_B = standard deviation of branch return
- σ_OA = standard deviation of other-asset returns

KEY INSIGHT: Geographic diversification can reduce overall risk exposure


--- BRANCH CLOSURE REGULATION ---
Recent regulation made closing US depository branches harder:
- FDIC Improvement Act of 1991
- Community Reinvestment Act of 1977

Branch role evolution today:
- Sales orientation

--- IN-STORE BRANCHING ---
- Located inside supermarkets, shopping centers, retail establishments
- Few employees, highly sales-oriented
- Much cheaper to build and maintain
- Become profitable ~12 months earlier than stand-alone facilities
- Operate longer hours
- Higher traffic flow than conventional branches

TELEPHONE BANKING & CALL CENTERS:

INTERNET BANKING — features include:

=== FINANCIAL-SERVICE FACILITIES OF THE FUTURE ===

- "Digital cash" will let customers be their own branches for some transactions

--- FINANCIAL HOLDING COMPANIES (FHCs) under GLB ---
Under Gramm-Leach-Bliley (GLB) Act:
- FHCs = special holding company offering broadest range of financial services
- Includes dealing in/underwriting securities and selling/underwriting insurance

In an FHC, each affiliated firm has its own:
- Capital
- Management
- Profits/losses separate from other affiliates
- Some protection against company-wide losses

Led to consolidation and convergence within the industry.

Possible affiliated firms within an FHC:
- Insurance Agencies (selling policies and insurance underwriting)
- Commercial Bank or Banks
- Security purchases/sales and Investment Banking (underwriting)
- Merchant Bank
- Travel Agencies and Other Affiliates
- Real Estate Development Company
